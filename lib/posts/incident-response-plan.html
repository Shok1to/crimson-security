A ransomware note shows up on a file server at 2 a.m. The IT lead has a 70-page incident response binder, and the questions that matter aren't in it. Who can approve taking the ERP system offline? Who calls outside counsel? Has anyone told the cyber insurer? Is paying the ransom even an option? Most plans don't fail because they're missing. They fail at the first executive decision because they're too technical, too long, or vague about who approves what.

The risk is not hypothetical. Statistics Canada data shows that almost 20% of small enterprises and 30% of medium enterprises had a cyber incident in 2017, and the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security's planning guidance says a plan should help organizations detect, respond, and recover quickly while limiting disruption, data loss, and reputational harm. A plan built for executives turns technical detection into fast business decisions you can defend on containment, disclosure, and recovery. This guide covers what to build, how to test it, and how to pick a Canadian partner who keeps it working.

What an Executive-Ready Plan Contains

Decision Rights and Escalation

Start by defining severity tiers by business impact, not by technical indicators. A Tier 1 event might be one compromised mailbox. A Tier 3 event stops revenue-generating systems or exposes regulated personal information. Each tier should name who gets notified and how quickly.

Next, fit the escalation chain onto a single page. That matrix should give named roles pre-approved authority to isolate systems, engage legal counsel, notify the cyber insurer, preserve evidence, approve executive communications, and escalate ransom decisions to the board. Response is a business process as much as a technical one. Federal incident response guidance stresses coordination across business owners, legal, procurement, and operations. If authority is clear before the incident, nobody hesitates during the most expensive hour of it.

Compliance Mapping and Tested Playbooks

Playbooks should line up with the obligations you actually have. Under PIPEDA, organizations must report breaches of security safeguards involving personal information when it is reasonable to believe there is a real risk of significant harm, and they must keep records of all breaches. The federal privacy guide for businesses lays out those duties. If you operate in Quebec, Law 25 adds provincial notification and record-keeping requirements, so a Quebec-specific decision path belongs in the playbook. Federally regulated financial institutions answer to OSFI B-13, which expects them to respond to cyber incidents quickly and effectively and to maintain continuous response capability.

A playbook you haven't tested is a guess. The Cyber Centre's ransomware playbook recommends tabletop exercises and simulated events to find weak points before a real breach does, and OSFI's B-13 guideline calls for scenario testing. We pair tabletops with penetration testing so leaders practise decisions against attack paths that really exist in their environment, then fix the gaps in order of business impact.
