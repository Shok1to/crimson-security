Most first SOC 2 audits stall because of missing evidence, untested controls, or a scope nobody fully agreed on. Weak technology is rarely the cause. A company can run modern infrastructure and still struggle in the audit if it can't show that its controls operated consistently over time.

A SOC 2 report is an attestation issued by a licensed CPA firm. It tells customers and partners that your controls line up with the AICPA Trust Services Criteria. Canadian SaaS and service companies selling into enterprise accounts in Canada and the US are asked for it more and more, because buyers use it as a standard artifact in vendor risk reviews. This guide covers the prerequisites, the order to work in, a worked example, and the mistakes that cause the most delay. The focus throughout is on prioritizing and fixing gaps, not on ticking off a generic checklist.

Prerequisites and Readiness Steps

Prerequisites Before You Start

Define scope first. Write down which systems are in scope, how customer data moves between them, which vendors and subservice providers support them, and which criteria apply. The AICPA Trust Services Criteria cover Security, Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality, and Privacy. Every engagement includes Security. You add the other categories based on what you promise customers. A company with uptime commitments in its contracts, for example, would usually add Availability.

Scope cuts both ways. If you scope too broadly, you multiply the evidence you have to collect. If you scope too narrowly, buyers may find the report doesn't answer their questions.

Next, name an executive sponsor, give every control a named owner, and set a budget that covers remediation, security testing, and auditor fees. Agree on a target observation window before any work starts. None of this is an AICPA requirement, but these four decisions largely determine whether readiness work finishes on time.

Sequenced Readiness Steps

1. Run a gap assessment. Go through the selected criteria control by control and record where you fall short. Rank each gap by its risk and by how much it affects the audit. Access reviews, logging and monitoring, change management, incident response, and vendor management tend to come first. Backup and recovery move up the list if Availability is in scope. The AICPA's SOC 2 examination guidance treats risk assessment as the basis for control design, so this ranking will hold up under audit.

2. Commission a penetration test and security risk assessment. Choose a qualified Canadian provider whose testers hold recognized credentials such as OffSec's OSCP or CREST certifications. Ask for a clear scope, written rules of engagement, and retesting after you remediate. The retest report is your proof that findings were closed before the observation window opened.

3. Formalize and test incident response. A written policy alone won't satisfy an auditor. Run a tabletop exercise, which is a facilitated walk-through of a realistic incident such as ransomware encrypting production systems, to check your escalation paths and decision rights. An incident response retainer can strengthen this control further. Look for 24/7 coverage, ransomware and forensics capability, and defined response SLAs. If you handle personal information, map your incident response steps to your obligations under Canada's federal privacy laws, including PIPEDA breach reporting.

4. Collect evidence continuously. Capture evidence as part of normal operations: access review records, change tickets, vendor reviews, tabletop outputs, test results, and documented exceptions. Evidence rebuilt after the fact is easy for auditors to spot.

5. Run a readiness review. Before you engage the CPA firm, check four things. Is each control clearly designed? Does it operate consistently? Is evidence mapped to it? Have exceptions been documented and fixed?

Worked Example and Pitfalls

Example: Canadian SaaS Company

Take a 60-person SaaS company in Toronto that handles customer data under PIPEDA. It wants a Type I report within four months and a Type II report after a six-month observation window.

The gap assessment finds that access reviews are informal and leave no record. The company introduces quarterly reviews, assigns an owner to each system, and keeps signed-off review records. Its penetration test turns up an exposed administrative interface and outdated libraries. Both are fixed and retested before the observation window opens. A tabletop exercise confirms that the IR retainer's escalation path works and that the response SLAs are realistic. Finally, the team maps each control to specific artifacts, so the auditor can test both design and operation without chasing anyone. As examination guidance makes clear, the Type II report depends on that evidence holding up across the entire period.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Over-scoping systems. Every system you add brings more evidence requests with it.

  • Treating policies as evidence. A policy shows what you intend to do. Auditors test what you actually did.

  • Leaving pen test findings open. Unresolved findings during the window weaken your vulnerability management controls.

  • No tested incident response. A plan that has never been exercised leaves an operational gap.

  • Ignoring vendor SOC reports. Oversight of third parties is part of your control environment, and the AICPA's commentary on maintaining SOC engagement standards reinforces that expectation.

  • Last-minute manual evidence collection. This is one of the most common causes of delay.

Bringing It Together

A successful first SOC 2 audit comes down to tight scoping, remediation ranked by risk, and controls you can prove were tested as well as written down. Start with a gap assessment and a penetration test, since together they show most of what needs fixing before the window opens. Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a first SOC 2 audit take?

Readiness typically takes two to six months. A Type I report covers a single point in time. A Type II report requires an observation window, usually between 3 and 12 months.

Is penetration testing required for SOC 2?

The framework does not explicitly require it. Auditors do commonly expect it as evidence for risk assessment and vulnerability management controls, especially at growing companies, and vendor risk reviewers often look for it alongside the report.

Do I need an incident response retainer?

No, it isn't required. A retainer does strengthen your incident response controls by guaranteeing expert support, defined SLAs, and forensics capability when a breach occurs.
